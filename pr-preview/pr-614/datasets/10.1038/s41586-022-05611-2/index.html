  • fig1e-velocity-timescales

    DLS dopamine fluctuations are significantly negatively correlated with translational velocity at short timescales (under 10 s), and the correlation reverses and becomes positive at longer timescales, for both dLight transient rates and binned amplitudes.

    These dopamine fluctuations (measured as both dLight transient rates and average binned amplitudes) were only weakly correlated with many aspects of movement kinematics ... but were significantly negatively correlated with translational velocity at short timescales (<10 s); this correlation reversed and became positive at longer timescales

    Results, Relating DLS dopamine to spontaneous behavior, paragraph 2; Figure 1e

    How it will be judged

    Per session, the z-scored dLight and the 2D speed are averaged (amplitude) or the rate of upward crossings of 1.96 is taken (transient rate) in non-overlapping bins of 0.25 to 59.75 s in 0.5-s steps, bins with fewer than the modal number of frames dropped, and Pearson r taken per session, then averaged over sessions. The null is 100 shuffles of the binned dLight within each session; a mean r outside the 2.5 to 97.5 percentiles of the shuffled means is significant. "At short timescales" can be read as (a) at every timescale below 10 s, or (b) at the shortest timescale (0.25 s). For one measure the claim holds when, under a reading, the mean r is significantly negative and at some timescale of 10 s or more it is significantly positive. Reproduced when it holds for both measures; partially reproduced for one.

  • fig1h-syllable-dlight-differs

    Right, z-scored average syllable-associated waveforms, sorted by peak fluorescence ... Kruskal–Wallis H test on average syllable-associated fluorescence amplitudes

    Figure 1h legend

    How it will be judged

Reproduced when P < 1e-25 and both values are within tolerance; partially reproduced when P < 0.05 but the P or a value misses; not reproduced when P >= 0.05.

  • fig1l-identity-not-decodable

    Random forest classifiers cannot predict which syllable is being expressed from the coincident dLight peak amplitude or waveform.

    Indeed, random forest classifiers were unable to predict which syllable was being expressed by the mouse at any moment based on the coincident dLight waveform amplitudes or shapes

    Results, DLS dopamine does not predict syllable identity, paragraph 2; Figure 1l

    How it will be judged

    Instances of the 37 named syllables are subsampled to 1,000 per syllable (seeded), and a random forest (100 trees) is trained with stratified fivefold cross-validation on (i) the peak amplitude alone and (ii) the 0 to 0.3 s waveform and its derivative. Chance is 1/37. The paper gives no accuracy; "unable to predict" is taken as held-out accuracy below twice chance (0.054). Reproduced when both feature sets are below it; partially reproduced when one is. Accuracy with shuffled labels is reported as the null.

  • fig2b-dlight-usage-across-syllables

    Syllables associated on average with more DLS dopamine in an experiment tend to be used more.

    those syllables that were on average associated with more DLS dopamine during a given experiment tended to be used more during that experiment

    Results, Dopamine predicts future syllable use and sequencing, paragraph 2; Figure 2b

    How it will be judged

    Per experiment, the mean syllable-associated dLight of instances longer than 0.3 s is z-scored across syllables; per mouse and syllable this value and the syllable count are averaged over experiments, keeping syllables with at least 100 instances in the mouse. A Huber regressor (epsilon 1.05) predicts count from dLight with fivefold cross-validation repeated 20 times; r is the Pearson correlation of held-out predictions with the data, and its null is 200 fits with dLight shuffled. Reproduced when r > 0 with one-sided shuffle P < 0.05.

  • fig2e-dlight-predicts-future-usage

    When an instance of a syllable coincides with a higher-amplitude dopamine transient, that syllable is used more over the following minutes.

    if a given instance of a syllable coincided with a relatively high amplitude dopamine transient, that syllable was then used more over the next several minutes

    Results, Dopamine predicts future syllable use and sequencing, paragraph 2; Figure 2c-g

    How it will be judged

    Per experiment and named syllable, for each instance its syllable-associated dLight and the count of the same syllable among the next n syllables, for n = 10 to 390 in steps of 20 (instances without n following syllables dropped). Pearson r is taken within each of five equal time segments of the session and averaged, then averaged over experiments per mouse and syllable, then over mouse-syllable pairs. The null is 100 shuffles of dLight within experiment, syllable and segment; one-sided P is the fraction of shuffled means at or above the observed one. Reproduced when the mean r is positive with P < 0.05 at every n from 10 to 190 (about two minutes); partially reproduced when it is so at n = 10 but not throughout; not reproduced otherwise.

  • fig3l-no-kinematic-effect

    Brief (250 ms) Opto-DA does not change movement at the moment of stimulation; no comparison between stimulation and catch trials in any mouse is significant.

    No comparisons between stimulation and catch trials in any of the mice were significant

    Figure 3l legend; Results, Syllable-associated Opto-DA influences behaviour, paragraph 5

    How it will be judged

    A claim of absence. Stimulation trials are pulse onsets; catch trials are online target onsets in the same sessions with no pulse within the next 0.5 s, aligned 0.17 s after onset (the median latency to stimulation). Per trial, the mean 2D speed from 0 to 1 s after alignment minus the mean over the preceding 1 s. Per Opto-DA mouse, a one-sided Mann-Whitney U test of stimulation above catch, P < 0.05 uncorrected as the legend gives. Reproduced when no Opto-DA mouse is significant; not reproduced when any is. Holm-corrected results and controls are shown as variants.
