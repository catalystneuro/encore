Time kinetics of the membrane potential at the cathode- and anode-facing poles of a cell induced by a train of 5 pulses at 833 kHz

Cricetulus aureus

Claims 6

From the paper Action spectra and mechanisms of (in) efficiency of bipolar electric pulses at electroporation · claims file · checked 2026-11-10: ready

  • fig7-unipolar-pole-polarization

    Unipolar 600 ns pulses depolarized the membrane quickly at the cathode-facing pole of the cell and hyperpolarized it in mirror image at the anode-facing pole.

    Unipolar nsEP caused fast membrane depolarization at the cathode-facing pole of the cell, and a mirroring hyperpolarization at the anode-facing pole.

    Results 3.5, paragraph 1; Figure 7

    How it will be judged

    FluoVolt emission at each pole of the 18 cells given unipolar pulses, processed as Methods 2.6 says (scaled to whole-cell emission, the two trials of each cell averaged, the mean of the 40 points before the pulse as 100%, percent difference from it). Which sign of emission change means depolarization is not given in the text; the cathodic pole change during the first pulse fixes it, and the anodic pole must then change with the opposite sign. Reproduced when, at the end of the first pulse (600 ns), the cathodic and anodic values differ from zero in opposite directions by a two-sided one-sample Student's t-test across cells at p < 0.05 (the Methods 2.5 test and threshold applied by this file, as the paper reports no test here), and the anodic change is of similar size (its magnitude within half to twice the cathodic, a tolerance of the file's for "mirroring").

  • fig7-charging-time-constant

    Fitting the cathodic pole response to the first unipolar pulse gave a charging time constant of 200 ns and a maximum polarization of 31% emission change (425 points, r = 0.93, p < 0.0001), and the discharge after it a time constant of 520 ns from 31.5% (425 points, r = 0.85, p < 0.0001).

    The best fit for membrane charging with an exponential function (using the first pulse in the unipolar pulse train) yielded the time constant of 200 ns and the maximum membrane polarization at 31% emission change (425 datapoints, correlation coefficient 0.93, p<0.0001). The best fit for the discharging portion of the curve yielded the same initial polarization (31.5% emission change) but, unexpectedly, a 2.5 times larger time constant of 520 ns (425 datapoints, correlation coefficient 0.85, p<0.0001

    Results 3.5, paragraph 2; Figure 7 (dotted lines, top panel) and legend

    How it will be judged

    Cathodic pole traces of the 18 unipolar cells, processed as in fig7-unipolar-pole-polarization. A single exponential (Fig. 7 legend) is fitted to the first pulse (0 to 600 ns, charging, A(1 - exp(-t/tau))) and to the interval from its end to the next pulse (600 to 1200 ns at 833 kHz, discharge, A exp(-t/tau)). The paper does not say which points were fitted. The 425 datapoints are close to 18 cells times 24 time steps of 25 ns (432), so the readings are (a) per-cell points pooled, the reading the count supports, and (b) the trace averaged over cells. These are method choices, run together. Reproduced when all four values are within tolerance and both correlations have p < 0.0001; partially reproduced when the two time constants are within tolerance and the discharge constant exceeds the charging constant, but an amplitude is not.

  • fig7-later-pulses-same-tau-difference

    Fits to the later pulses of the unipolar train gave the same kind of difference between charging and discharging time constants as the first pulse.

    Fitting the membrane potential for the next pulses in the train consistently produced similar differences between the charging and discharging time constants (data not shown).

    Results 3.5, paragraph 2

    How it will be judged

    The paper shows no data for this finding. The fits of fig7-charging-time-constant, under the same readings, are repeated for pulses 2 to 5 (each pulse and the interval after it; after pulse 5 the discharge is fitted over 600 ns, the same span as for the others). "Similar differences" is read as the discharge constant exceeding the charging constant, with a ratio within 10% of the paper's 2.5 (2.25 to 2.75) as one reading and within 10% of the first pulse's ratio as found here as another; either suffices. Reproduced when this holds for all four later pulses; partially reproduced when the discharge constant is larger for all four but the ratio holds for fewer.

  • fig7-bipolar-overshoot

    Bipolar pulses caused the same initial polarization as unipolar ones during the first phase, followed by a faster discharge and an overshoot into the opposite polarity.

    Bipolar nsEP caused the same initial polarization during the first phase of the pulse, followed by a faster discharge and an overshoot into the opposite polarity.

    Results 3.5, paragraph 1; Figure 7

    How it will be judged

    Cathodic pole traces as in fig7-unipolar-pole-polarization, for the 17 bipolar and 18 unipolar cells. Reproduced when (i) at the end of the first phase (600 ns) the bipolar and unipolar cell values do not differ by the paper's two-sided Student's t-test at p < 0.05 (Methods 2.5; unpaired, as they are different cells), a claim of absence judged on that test, with the paper's error bar gap rule (Methods 2.5) shown as a variant; (ii) after the first phase the bipolar mean trace returns to the resting level sooner than the unipolar trace; and (iii) during the second phase (600 to 1200 ns) the bipolar mean goes below the resting level, the cell values at its minimum differing from zero by a two-sided one-sample Student's t-test at p < 0.05 (the Methods 2.5 test and threshold applied by this file, as the paper reports no test here).

  • fig7-bipolar-alternation

    In 833 kHz trains, bipolar pulses caused the membrane potential to alternate repeatedly between positive and negative values.

    Bipolar pulses caused repetitive alternation between positive and negative membrane potentials, which could facilitate pore closure beyond just returning the potential to the base level.

    Results 3.5, paragraph 3; Figure 7

    How it will be judged

    Cathodic pole traces of the 17 bipolar cells, processed as in fig7-unipolar-pole-polarization, with the sign of depolarization fixed there. For each of the five pulses, the value at the end of the first phase and the minimum during the second phase are tested against zero by a two-sided one-sample Student's t-test across cells at p < 0.05 (the Methods 2.5 test and threshold applied by this file, as the paper reports no test here). Reproduced when, for every pulse, the first phase value is of the depolarizing sign and the second phase minimum of the opposite sign, both significant; partially reproduced when this holds for some pulses.

  • fig7-summation-without-buildup

    With 600 ns unipolar pulses at 833 kHz, each pulse charged the membrane to the same peak, with no stepwise build-up; the pulses merged into a continuous sawtooth polarization.

    Although charging by the subsequent pulses in unipolar pulse trains started with the partially charged membrane, it reached the same peak potential. A stepwise build-up of the peak membrane potential ... did not occur in our experiments with 833-kHz, 600-ns unipolar pulses.

    Results 3.5, paragraph 3; Figure 7

    How it will be judged

    Cathodic pole traces of the unipolar cells as in fig7-unipolar-pole-polarization. The peak of each pulse is the value at its end. No build-up is a claim of absence, judged on the paper's test as written, the two-sided Student's t-test at p < 0.05 (Methods 2.5), unpaired (opening comment), comparing each of the peaks of pulses 2 to 5 with the first; the paired test is shown as a variant. The continuous polarization is judged by the trace before each of pulses 2 to 5 staying above the resting level, its value there differing from zero by a two-sided one-sample Student's t-test across cells at p < 0.05 (the Methods 2.5 test applied by this file). Reproduced when no later peak differs from the first and the trace stays above rest before every later pulse; not reproduced when a later peak differs from the first.
