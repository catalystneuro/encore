High-Degree Neurons Feed Cortical Computations

Nicholas M. Timme, Shinya Ito, Maxym Myroshnychenko, Sunny Nigam, Masanori Shimono, Fang-Chin Yeh, Paweł Hottowy, Alan M Litke, John M. Beggs

Outcomes of 14 claims

  • ◐4 partially reproduced
  • 3 not reproduced

Summary: Most of the paper's network findings held, on transfer entropy networks rebuilt from the spike times because the paper's networks are not deposited. High-degree neurons were far more common than in random networks, weights spanned orders of magnitude, and a transmitter's out-degree correlated positively with its receivers' synergy in 42 of 49 recordings and time scales. In-degree correlations leaned slightly negative, and the pooled rise of synergy with out-degree did not appear. In the model the degree-modified rule matched the data's correlations most closely but gave the lowest signal-conditioned mutual information of four rules, where the paper reports the highest. The rebuilt significance test passes more chance connections than nominal, and high-degree neurons here are mostly high-rate ones.

    Results, Connection Weight and Degree Distributions, paragraph 4; Figure 4B and 4D

    Full networks: 10.2% (1.6 to 6.4 ms) and 14.3% (3.5 to 14 ms) of neurons have a total degree above the 99.9th percentile of matched random networks (100 per recording), 102 and 143 times 0.001; largest real total degree 373 and 517 against 84 and 147. Sub-networks: 7.1% and 6.6% of neuron slots above the random 99.9th percentile (one random network per sub-network); largest 53 and 52 against 14 and 13. Both readings meet the criterion, and so does every variant (strict rule, bins t-1 and t-2, random sub-network connections, the stricter threshold). Under the tie-counting rule degree rises with firing rate (median Spearman 0.75 and 0.81 for total degree), so a high-degree neuron here is mostly a high-rate neuron.

    How the test differed from the paper’s method

    • The TE networks are rebuilt from the deposited spike times; the paper reused the networks of its reference [44] (Timme et al. 2014, PLoS One 9 e115764), which are not deposited. Bins of 1.6 and 3.5 ms, past states the OR of two bins, a 7-bin uniform jitter, 5000 surrogates and fewer than 5 exceeding are taken from [44], Table 1 and Transfer Entropy Analysis, paragraphs 3 and 6, as the paper directs (Methods, Effective Connectivity Detection, paragraphs 1 and 4).
    • Assumed that a surrogate whose TE equals the real TE counts as exceeding it. [44] takes as the p-value the percentage of surrogates 'larger than' the real TE (Transfer Entropy Analysis, paragraph 6), and M-SIG says 'larger'. Taken literally, a pair whose counts the jitter cannot change ties in every surrogate and is called significant. On circularly shifted trains, where every pair is independent, the strict rule calls 10.8% of pairs significant and the tie-counting rule 0.32%, against the 0.1% of the paper's p < 0.001, so the strict rule is not the test the paper states. The strict rule is run as a variant. Multiplied by the number of pairs, the tie-counting rule's rate gives 8% to 30% of the significant connections of the five tested recordings as expected by chance (pooled 15.8%). No stricter count of the 5000 surrogates reaches 0.1%; the strictest, no surrogate at or above the real TE, gives 0.20% and 13.2%, and is run as the variant 'calibrated'.
    • Assumed which two bins form the past state. [44]'s text writes bins t-d and t-d-1 (with d = 1, bins t-1 and t-2), while its Table 1 and the paper give a delay window of 1 to 4 bins, which bins t-2 and t-3 give. Bins t-2 and t-3 are used; bins t-1 and t-2 are run as a variant.
    • For pairs whose jitter moves more than 50 relevant sender spikes, the surrogate counts are drawn from a normal distribution with their exact mean and covariance, not by jittering every spike, to fit the time limit. On DataSet1 and DataSet7 at both time scales the approximation and the full jitter share 83% to 89% of the connections either finds under the tie rule (95.7% to 99.2% under the strict rule), the approximation finding 0.1% to 8.2% more. The number of past states equal to 1 is held at its real value in each surrogate, as [44] states the jitter preserved it.
    • Sub-networks follow [44], Topology Analysis - Sub-Networks, paragraph 2 (50 random neurons, unconnected ones replaced from the pool, the 75 strongest connections by normalized TE kept; reading (a) of M-SUB), and a group with fewer than 75 significant connections is redrawn, as M-SUB says. Within 5000 draws four recordings give fewer than 500 sub-networks at 1.6 to 6.4 ms (DataSet2 6, DataSet5 0, DataSet10 380, DataSet17 148); these are used as they are. Reading (b), 75 connections at random, is run as a variant.
    • The variants (strict rule, bins t-1 and t-2, random sub-network connections, the stricter threshold) use 100 sub-networks per recording and 20 combinations per n, for time.

  • openasked 2026-11-06about fig5a-in-degree-uncorrelated

    M-PID takes the redundancy as the specific information of each source about each state of I_F within each state of I_P, the minimum over sources, weighted by p(i_F, i_P). Eq 8 of the version of record takes, for each state of I_F, the specific information of the source together with I_P minus that of I_P alone, the minimum over sources, weighted by p(i_F), so the minimum is taken once per i_F, not once per (i_F, i_P). Should M-PID follow Eq 8? The same applies to every claim that uses synergy (fig5b-out-degree-positive, fig5ab-correlations-beyond-null, fig5cd-pooled-trends, table1-pid-examples, fig7c-degree-modified-rule-matches).

    Passage. Materials and Methods, Multivariate Transfer Entropy and Computation, Eq 8, as read from the version of record's JATS XML, "Redundancy({J,K} -> I) = I_min(I_F; J_P, K_P | I_P) = sum over i_F of p(i_F) min over R in {J_P, K_P} of I_spec(I_F = i_F; R | I_P) = sum over i_F of p(i_F) min over R in {J_P, K_P} [I_spec(I_F = i_F; R, I_P) - I_spec(I_F = i_F; I_P)]", with Eqs 9 and 10 defining the two specific informations.
