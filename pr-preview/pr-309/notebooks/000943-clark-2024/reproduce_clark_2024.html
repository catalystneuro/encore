Clark and Nolan (2024) on dandiset 000943¶

Paper. Clark H, Nolan MF (2024). Task-anchored grid cell firing is selectively associated with successful path integration-dependent behaviour. eLife 12:RP89356. doi:10.7554/eLife.89356. The claims file was written from the version of record (reviewed preprint version 3, 28 March 2024, full text from Europe PMC PMC10977970) with its supplementary files: the summary table of recorded cells per mouse and the MDAR checklist.

Dandiset. DANDI:000943, version 0.240614.0754. The paper cites no DANDI version: its data statement points to Edinburgh DataShare (doi:10.7488/ds/7684). 0.240614.0754 is the only published version, so it is pinned. The draft has the same 734 assets and the same total size (checked below), so no other version could match the claims file better.

What the claims need and what the dandiset holds. Every one of the 31 claims rests on single units: grid cells are classified from the units' open arena firing, and every later claim uses those cells or their firing on the virtual track. The paper sorted spikes with MountainSort (Methods, Spike sorting: "Spikes were isolated from electrophysiological data using an automated pipeline based on MountainSort (v0.11.5 and dependencies) ... Recordings from the open-field and virtual reality tasks were concatenated for spike sorting"). The dandiset holds only the raw tetrode signal and the position series of each session; no file has a units table or any processed data (shown below). Testing any claim therefore means spike sorting the raw signal of every recording day, about 976 GB. This notebook surveys the dandiset, checks its description against the values, measures how fast the raw signal streams, and from that gives the estimate on which every claim is blocked as too_large.

The notes say that "in the published journal article only sessions with 200cm track lengths are used", that a stop is registered when speed drops below 4.7 cm/s, computed over the previous 100 ms, and the experiment description gives "a 100 x 100 cm open field" (the notes themselves describe the arena only as "a metal box with a square floor area"). Both texts are the same in the VR and the open arena file, both of M1. These are the sources of the track length, stop speed and arena size used below.

The dandiset's metadata lists 12 subjects and only ElectrodeGroup and ElectricalSeries as variables measured, which the files bear out: each of the 734 files holds the raw 16-channel signal at 30 kHz and a position series, and nothing else. There is no units table, no processing module and no trials table in any file, so the spike sorting, the units' quality metrics, the reward times and the trial table that the paper's analysis uses are not in the dandiset. The position description, identical in every file, names position, speed, angle, trials and trial types, which describes neither the three columns of the VR files (no speed or angle column) nor the nine columns of the open arena files.

Shared clock of the raw signal and the position series¶

The files hold no spikes and no trial table, so the shared clock is checked between the raw signal and the position series, both starting at 0 s. Multi-unit activity (threshold crossings, as in the helper) is counted in 100 ms bins and compared with running speed: in the medial entorhinal cortex activity rises with running speed, so if the two series share a clock the correlation peaks at zero lag and the contrast between running and stopped bins is largest with the labels unshifted. Six VR sessions (one per mouse, six mice) and three open arena sessions are checked, five minutes from 300 s each.

Why every claim is blocked: the size of spike sorting¶

Every claim needs single units, which the dandiset does not hold. To make them as the paper did, the raw signal of each recording day (the open arena and VR files of the day together) would be streamed, filtered, whitened and sorted with MountainSort, then curated by isolation, noise overlap and peak SNR (Methods, Spike sorting). The cell below measures how fast the raw signal streams from the archive here, with 16 processes each reading 20 s of signal (about 19 MB) from a different file, and from that gives a lower bound on the time to read the raw signal of the recording days of the 239 paired 200 cm sessions of the nine mice. These are a superset of the paper's 215 sessions, which the dandiset does not mark, so the size is also scaled by 215/239 as an estimate for the paper's sessions alone. Sorting time comes on top of it and is not measured. The limit compared with is the protocol's: "CI fails a notebook over sixty" minutes (docs/protocol.md, Author, step 3, "Keep it fast"). The bound alone exceeds it, so a timed MountainSort run, which would only add to it, was not set up.

How the analysis differs from the paper's. No analysis of the claims was run. The paper's analysis starts from units sorted from the raw signal with MountainSort; the dandiset holds the raw signal and positions only, and sorting about 1 TB of raw signal, of which the reading alone takes more than the notebook limit (above), cannot be done here.   • No file holds sorted units, a trials table, reward times or any processed data; every claim of the paper needs units.
