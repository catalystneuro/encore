Reproducing Welch et al. (2025) from dandiset 001863¶

Paper. Welch AC, Gonzales-Hess N, Tarvin T, Connor S, Smear M (2025). The impact of a psychedelic drug on olfactory search behavior by mice. bioRxiv doi:10.1101/2025.07.09.663970. The claims file was written from the full text of the preprint, version 1 (its JATS XML), which has its supplemental figure legends in the text and no separate supplement.

Dandiset. DANDI:001863, version 0.260806.2109. The paper cites no DANDI version (it predates the dandiset). This is the only published version; the draft has the same 44 assets and size, so the published version is pinned.

What the dandiset holds. 44 NWB files, one DOI and one saline session for each of 22 mice: the trial table, DeepLabCut tracking of 8 keypoints, the sniff cycles detected from the nasal thermistor (31 sessions) and an unnamed event series that the authors' conversion script and figure code identify as the head-twitch (HTR) times (32 sessions). The reward-port entries and exits of Figure 2 are not in it.

Outside inputs. The authors' code, github.com/Smear-Lab/Olfactory-search-on-psychedelics at commit aa80af0a153c6bf099e5668d27c9d88e2bfc033f, which the claims file cites for its method details. It also names the event series as HTR times (claim htr-event-link).

The ITI observed between trials is longer than the set ITI, as it includes the time to poke into the initiation port. In the five first-cohort mice 347 trials have a set ITI of 16 to 25 s, beyond the Methods' 1 to 15 s; the set ITI is not used by any claim. The session notes count correct and total odor trials by side, and in all 34 later-cohort sessions they equal the table's counts with side 2 as left and side 1 as right; the files do not say which side is which. false_alarm is 1 in 6 of 3261 trials, as the paper says false reports were rare.

Do the tracking frames share the trial table's clock?¶

The files give the tracking no timestamps: each series has a starting time of 0 and a rate that the conversion script estimated as the number of frames over the last trial's end time (its comments call it an approximation). The paper's code places frame i at i / 80 s. A trial starts at the initiation port and ends when the mouse reaches the odor side at the far end of the arena, so the nose's x at the end of a trial minus its x at the start (first cohort flipped) shows whether frames and trials are aligned. A rate and offset are fitted for each session (ds.fit_clock), and refitted on each half of the session.

With the code's clock (frame i at i / 80 s) the nose moves by a median of about 0 px between the start and the end of a trial: the frames the code labels as within a trial are not the trial's frames. The file's own rate does no better. A linear clock fitted to each session moves the nose by hundreds of pixels toward the odor end over a trial, and the same offset fits both halves of most sessions. The fitted rate is near 80.5 frames/s in the later cohort (the 80 frames/s of the code drifts by about 0.5 x 2400 / 80, some 15 s, over a session, more than a trial lasts) and 72 to 75 frames/s in the first cohort, whose videos hold fewer frames than 40 min at 80 frames/s. The sessions printed as weak are not established; besides a weak or unstable fit, this flags a fit whose implied video does not cover the trials or runs on more than 2 min past the last trial (2204 DOI, fitted at 71.0 frames/s, would end 311 s after it, and 2174 saline 165 s after), and leaves 30 of 44 sessions with a good fit. Shifting the trials by 30 s under the fitted clock removes the displacement, so the fit is a response to the trials and not an offset.

This is a finding about the dataset: the tracking carries no frame times and the stated rate is an estimate that does not align it with the trials. The claims file fixes the code's clock for the analyses that split frames by trial period (speed, HTR maps); they are run with it, as the paper did, and again with the fitted clock as a variant. The nose keypoint is below the 0.95 likelihood cut in most frames of many sessions, so most nose positions are interpolated, as the code does; the interpolated fraction does not differ by condition (higher on DOI in 11 of 22 mice, median difference -0.003).

R is not available here, so the signed-rank p-values of ds.r_signrank are checked against an exact null distribution counted directly (the number of subsets of the ranks 1 to n with each sum, as R's psignrank). def exact_signrank_p(a, b):
    """Two-sided exact signed-rank p from the counted null distribution (no ties, no zeros)."""
    d = (np.asarray(a, float) - np.asarray(b, float))
    d = d[~np.isnan(d)]
    r = stats.rankdata(np.abs(d))
    assert len(np.unique(r)) == len(r) and (d != 0).all()
    n = len(d)
    v = int(r[d > 0].sum())
    counts = np.zeros(n * (n + 1) // 2 + 1, dtype=object)
    counts[0] = 1
    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        counts[k:] = counts[k:] + counts[:-k].copy()
    total = 2 ** n
    lower = sum(counts[: v + 1]) / total
    upper = sum(counts[v:]) / total
    return v, float(min(1.0, 2 * min(lower, upper)))

How the analysis differs from the paper's. Statistics are computed in Python with R's defaults reproduced; the permutation tests draw other random permutations than R's. The Fisher-Pitman p-values of the per-period tests are resampled for reading (a) only. For the per-mouse KS tests the percentile exclusion is assumed to be per session (reading a). The occupancy measures are the claims file's readings of the paper's prediction, and the arena box is assumed here. Frames are split into trial periods by the code's 80 frames/s clock, with a fitted clock as a variant.

What was found about the dataset. The reward-port entries and exits of Figure 2 are not in it. The tracking has no frame times; its stated rate is an estimate, and neither it nor the code's 80 frames/s aligns the frames with the trial table, while a fitted clock (about 80.5 frames/s in the later cohort, 72 to 75 in the first) does in 30 of 44 sessions.
